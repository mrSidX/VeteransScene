Copyright & Content Guide

What our team needs to know about using content legally on YouTube

Why This Matters

As Veteran's Scene grows and our team creates content for YouTube, understanding copyright is essential. A single copyright strike can limit our channel's features, and three strikes within 90 days results in channel termination.

The good news: copyright law provides significant protections for commentary, criticism, education, and news reporting. As a veterans-focused channel discussing military topics, current events, and veteran experiences, much of what we do falls into protected categories. But we need to do it correctly.

Protected

Commentary, criticism, parody, education, news reporting

Careful

Music, movie clips, TV footage, branded content

Avoid

Full songs, entire episodes, unmodified copyrighted works

Fair Use — 17 U.S.C. § 107

Fair use is a legal doctrine that permits limited use of copyrighted material without permission from the copyright holder. It exists specifically to protect free speech, commentary, education, and creative expression.

"The fair use of a copyrighted work, including such use by reproduction in copies [...] for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, or research, is not an infringement of copyright."

— 17 U.S.C. § 107

2023 Update: Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith

The Supreme Court significantly tightened fair use analysis in 2023. The ruling clarified that even highly altered works are not automatically transformative if they serve the same purpose as the original. A new work must have a "fundamentally different and new" purpose or character — not just different aesthetics. This means: adding commentary, criticism, or educational context is more important than ever.

The Four Factors

Courts weigh all four factors together. No single factor is decisive.

1

Purpose and Character of the Use

Is the new work transformative? Does it add new meaning, commentary, or context? Commercial use weighs against fair use, but is not disqualifying.

Favors Fair Use:

  • Adding commentary or criticism
  • Using a clip to make a new point
  • Parody and satire
  • Educational or news purposes

Weighs Against:

  • Reposting content as-is
  • Using content purely for entertainment
  • No new commentary or meaning added
  • Direct commercial exploitation
2

Nature of the Copyrighted Work

Factual works (news footage, documentaries, government reports) get less protection than highly creative works (movies, music, novels). This factor generally favors us when discussing news, military operations, and factual events.

3

Amount and Substantiality Used

Use only what's necessary to make your point. Less is better, but quality matters too — using the "heart" of a work weighs against fair use even if the amount is small.

Rule of Thumb for Our Content:

  • Use short clips (5-30 seconds) when discussing video content
  • Show only what's needed to support your commentary
  • Never play an entire song, episode, or article
  • Pause and comment frequently when showing clips
4

Effect on the Market

Would your use serve as a substitute for the original? If viewers watch your video instead of the original, that weighs against fair use. Commentary and criticism generally don't substitute for the original.

Protected Categories in Detail

Commentary & Criticism

You may use portions of copyrighted works to comment on or critique them. This is the strongest fair use category for YouTube creators.

Example: Showing a 15-second clip from a news broadcast about veterans, then pausing to discuss what's wrong or right about their reporting. Your commentary is the primary content; the clip supports it.

Key: You must actually be commenting on the content you're showing. Playing a clip just because it's entertaining, without substantive commentary, is not criticism.

Parody & Satire

Parody (mocking the original work itself) has strong fair use protection. Satire (using a work to mock something else) has weaker protection.

Parody example: Creating a humorous version of a military recruitment ad that comments on the ad itself. (Strong protection — Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, 1994)

Satire example: Using a copyrighted song as background while making fun of something unrelated to the song. (Weaker protection — you could have used a different song)

News Reporting

Reporting on current events allows use of copyrighted material to illustrate the story. This applies to independent media and YouTube channels, not just traditional news outlets.

Example: Showing a brief clip of a congressional hearing about VA benefits while reporting on and discussing the hearing's outcomes.

Limit: Use only what's necessary to tell the story. Don't rebroadcast entire segments.

Education & Scholarship

Educational use is favored, but it's not a blanket pass. The use must be genuinely educational, not just labeled as such.

Example: Using historical photos and brief documentary clips to teach viewers about a specific military operation or veteran benefit program.

Not educational: Labeling a reaction video "educational" without providing actual educational content.

Government & Military Content

Under 17 U.S.C. § 105, works created by U.S. federal government employees as part of their official duties are not eligible for copyright protection and are in the public domain. This is a significant advantage for our content.

What You CAN Use Freely

FREE TO USE

DoD Photos & Videos

Photos and videos taken by military photographers (DVIDS, defense.gov, military branch websites) as part of their official duties.

FREE TO USE

Congressional Hearings

C-SPAN footage of congressional proceedings, hearings, and floor speeches. These are government works.

FREE TO USE

Government Reports & Documents

VA reports, GAO studies, DoD publications, executive orders, federal regulations, and court opinions.

FREE TO USE

NASA Content

Photos, videos, and audio from NASA (unless specifically noted as from a contractor or third party).

Important Restrictions on Government Content

No Implied Endorsement

You may not use government or military content in a way that implies the DoD, any military branch, or the U.S. government endorses your content, products, or views. This is a DoD policy requirement, not copyright law.

Military Branch Logos & Insignia

While not copyrighted, official military seals, emblems, and logos are protected under 18 U.S.C. § 506 and various DoD regulations. Using them in a way that suggests official endorsement or in commercial merchandise is prohibited. Editorial/educational use in videos discussing the military is generally acceptable.

Privacy & Identifiable Persons

Even public domain military photos may feature identifiable service members. Be mindful of context, especially with content related to special operations, casualties, or sensitive missions. OPSEC (Operational Security) considerations may apply.

Contractor-Created Content

Not all content on .mil or .gov websites is government-created. Content produced by contractors may be copyrighted. Look for photo/video credits. If credited to a contractor or civilian, it may not be public domain.

Best Sources for Government Content

  • ✓ DVIDS (dvidshub.net) — Defense Visual Information Distribution Service. Primary source for DoD imagery.
  • ✓ defense.gov — Official DoD photos, press releases, and publications.
  • ✓ Branch Websites — army.mil, navy.mil, af.mil, marines.mil, coastguard.mil
  • ✓ National Archives (archives.gov) — Historical military photos, documents, and film.
  • ✓ VA.gov — Veterans Affairs publications, infographics, and reports.

Public Domain

Public domain works have no copyright restrictions. You can use them freely, modify them, and even monetize content that includes them.

Category Public Domain When
U.S. Government Works Immediately (17 U.S.C. § 105)
Published before 1929 Now public domain (as of 2024)
Published 1929-1977 without copyright notice Public domain (notice was required)
Published 1929-1963 with notice but not renewed Public domain (renewal was required)
Works by authors who died 70+ years ago Life + 70 years (current rule for post-1978 works)
Dedicated by owner When explicitly released (CC0, public domain dedication)

For our purposes: Most WWII-era and earlier military footage, photos published before 1929, and all U.S. government-produced content are public domain. Korean War, Vietnam, and later conflicts — government-produced content is public domain; news network footage is not.

Music & Audio

Music is the #1 source of copyright claims on YouTube. Even a few seconds of a popular song playing in the background can trigger Content ID. This section is critical.

Safe Music Sources

SAFE

YouTube Audio Library

Free music and sound effects provided by YouTube. Some require attribution in the video description. Available in YouTube Studio.

SAFE

Royalty-Free Libraries

Services like Epidemic Sound, Artlist, or Pixabay Music. Pay a license fee (or use free tiers) and use the music without claims. Read their specific license terms.

SAFE

Creative Commons Music

Music released under CC licenses (see Creative Commons section below). Always check which CC license applies and follow its requirements.

SAFE

Original Compositions

Music you or a team member created. If someone else made it for you, get a written agreement confirming you have rights to use it.

What About Using Popular Songs?

Using copyrighted music requires licenses. For a YouTube video, you would typically need:

  • Synchronization (Sync) License — Permission from the songwriter/publisher to pair their music with visual content
  • Master Use License — Permission from the record label to use their specific recording

These licenses are expensive and complex. For our channel, do not use copyrighted music as background, intro/outro, or ambiance. Use the safe sources listed above.

Exception: Brief clips of music used for direct commentary/criticism about that music may qualify as fair use (e.g., discussing a military-themed song and playing a 10-second sample while analyzing its lyrics).

YouTube Copyright Policies

YouTube has its own copyright enforcement system that operates independently from the courts. Something can be legal fair use but still get flagged by YouTube's automated system. Understanding both the law and YouTube's platform rules is essential.

Copyright Strike vs. Content ID Claim

Copyright Strike

  • Filed manually by a copyright holder via DMCA takedown
  • Video is removed from YouTube
  • 3 strikes in 90 days = channel terminated
  • 1 strike: lose access to some features for 90 days
  • Can be appealed via counter-notification (DMCA process)
  • Counter-notification is a legal declaration under penalty of perjury

Content ID Claim

  • Automated system matches audio/video against a database
  • Video stays up (usually)
  • Does NOT count as a strike
  • Copyright holder may: monetize your video, track views, or block in some countries
  • Can be disputed if you believe it's fair use
  • If dispute rejected, you can appeal (then it may become a strike if the holder files a takedown)

Content ID System

Content ID is YouTube's automated fingerprinting system. Copyright holders upload reference files, and YouTube scans every upload against this database.

What Triggers Content ID

Almost Always Triggers:

  • Any recognizable music (even humming)
  • TV or movie audio/video
  • Sports broadcasts
  • Music playing in the background of your recording

Rarely Triggers:

  • Original spoken content (interviews, discussions)
  • Government/public domain footage
  • Royalty-free music from proper libraries
  • Your own original footage

Dispute Process

1

Content ID Claim Received

You receive a claim. Video usually stays up but may be monetized by the claimant.

2

You Dispute (if fair use applies)

Select "Fair Use" as your reason. Explain how your use is transformative. The claimant has 30 days to respond.

3

Claimant Responds

They can release the claim, uphold it, or take no action (claim released after 30 days).

4

Appeal (if upheld)

You can appeal once more. If the claimant still disagrees, they must file a formal DMCA takedown (which becomes a copyright strike) or release the claim.

Team Policy: Before disputing any claim, discuss it with the team. Only dispute claims where we have a strong fair use case. Frivolous disputes can escalate to strikes.

Creative Commons Licenses

Creative Commons (CC) licenses let creators share their work with specific permissions. Look for CC-licensed content as a great source of usable material.

License Can Use? Requirements
CC0 (Public Domain) Yes No requirements. Use however you want.
CC BY (Attribution) Yes Credit the creator in your video description.
CC BY-SA (ShareAlike) Careful Credit creator + your video must use the same license.
CC BY-NC (NonCommercial) Careful Credit creator. Cannot monetize the video if using this content.
CC BY-ND (NoDerivatives) Careful Credit creator. Cannot modify or remix the content.
CC BY-NC-ND Restrictive Credit creator. No monetization. No modifications. Very limited use.

Best for our channel: CC0 and CC BY content. These give us maximum flexibility including monetization. Always credit CC BY creators in the video description.

Penalties & Consequences

YouTube Consequences

1 STRIKE

Warning. Lose ability to upload, post, or live stream for 1 week. Strike expires after 90 days.

2 STRIKES

Cannot upload, post, or live stream for 2 weeks. Both strikes must expire (90 days each).

3 STRIKES

Channel permanently terminated. All videos removed. Cannot create new channels.

Legal Consequences (U.S. Copyright Law)

Copyright infringement can result in civil and criminal penalties under U.S. law:

  • Statutory Damages: $750 to $30,000 per work infringed (up to $150,000 for willful infringement)
  • Actual Damages: Lost profits suffered by the copyright holder
  • Attorney's Fees: Losing party may pay the other side's legal costs
  • Criminal Penalties: Up to 5 years imprisonment for willful infringement for commercial gain (rare for YouTube creators, but possible)

For our channel, the realistic risk is YouTube strikes and potential civil claims. Following this guide minimizes that risk significantly.

Common Myths — Debunked

"I gave credit, so it's fine."

Attribution does NOT create fair use. Giving credit is polite and good practice, but it has zero legal effect on whether your use is infringing.

"I wrote 'no copyright infringement intended.'"

This disclaimer has absolutely no legal effect. Courts and YouTube both ignore it entirely.

"It's only 30 seconds, so it's fair use."

There is no "X seconds is always safe" rule. Even a few seconds can infringe if it's the "heart" of the work. Conversely, longer uses can be fair if highly transformative. Context matters.

"I'm not monetizing, so it's not infringement."

Non-commercial use weighs in favor of fair use, but it doesn't make infringement legal. You can be sued for non-monetized content.

"It was on the internet, so it's free to use."

Being publicly accessible does not mean public domain. Almost everything on the internet is copyrighted by default. Only works explicitly released as public domain or under permissive licenses are free to use.

"It's a reaction video, so it's automatically fair use."

Simply watching and reacting with minimal commentary is weak fair use. You need substantive analysis, criticism, or commentary. The more you add, the stronger your case.

Guest Appearances & Releases

When interviewing veterans or featuring guests, copyright is generally not the concern — but consent and privacy are. Every guest should agree to being recorded and published.

Best Practices

✓

Get a Release Form

Have each guest sign a simple release granting permission to record and publish the interview on YouTube, podcast platforms, and promotional materials. This avoids future disputes.

✓

Verbal Consent on Recording

At minimum, get verbal confirmation on camera/recording that the guest consents to the interview being published. This creates a record even without a written form.

!

Sensitive Topics

Be mindful of PTSD-related topics. YouTube's community guidelines restrict excessively graphic content. Let guests set boundaries on what they're comfortable discussing publicly.

!

Classified Information

If a veteran shares potentially classified information during an interview, do not publish that portion. While civilians are generally not bound by classification rules, the veteran could face consequences, and publishing could invite government action.
